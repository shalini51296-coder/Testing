# Kids Learning App (ages 4 to 10): Research Foundations

**Phase 1 of the project: the evidence base, before any design or code.**
The learner is 4 years 2 months now. The app should grow with her to about age 10 (Nursery/LKG through Class 4–5).

Rule used throughout: a practice goes in only if it is supported by **meta-analyses, randomized trials, or national expert panels**. Popular claims that fail that test are listed as such (Section 2). Each claim cites its source (full list in Section 11).

---

## 1. Bottom line (read this if nothing else)

1. **"Raising IQ" through games is not supported. Building knowledge and skills is.** Commercial brain training and working-memory games improve the trained task but do not transfer to general intelligence (Simons et al., 2016; Melby-Lervåg & Hulme, 2013; Sala & Gobet, 2017). What reliably raises measured IQ is **education itself**: about **1–5 IQ points per extra year of schooling** (Ritchie & Tucker-Drob, 2018). The app's job is to be excellent education, not "brain training".
2. **Five areas give the most long-term return from age 4 to 10:**
   - **oral language and vocabulary**
   - **systematic phonics, then fluent reading**
   - **early number sense, which predicts later achievement better than early reading skills do** (Duncan et al., 2007)
   - **spatial reasoning, one of the few cognitive abilities that training improves with lasting effects and some transfer** (Uttal et al., 2013)
   - **executive function: attention, working memory and self-control** (Diamond & Lee, 2011; Moffitt et al., 2011)
3. **How children learn matters as much as what they learn.** The best-supported learning mechanisms are:
   - retrieval practice
   - spaced repetition
   - interleaving
   - mastery-based progression
   - immediate, informative feedback
   - curiosity triggered by information gaps

   (Dunlosky et al., 2013; Dehaene, 2020)
4. **An app for a 4-year-old must be "active, engaged, meaningful and socially interactive"** (Hirsh-Pasek et al., 2015). It works best with a parent in the loop. Keep it to **at most 1 hour a day of total screen time at age 3–4** (WHO, 2019), and keep handwriting, blocks and books physical.
5. **Avoid manipulative game design.** That means no loot boxes, streak guilt, ads or token economies that replace the joy of the task. Extrinsic rewards for activities a child already enjoys can reduce motivation (Lepper et al., 1973; Deci et al., 1999). Manipulative design is common in children's apps (Radesky et al., 2022).

## 2. Separating evidence from myth

| Claim | Verdict | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Brain-training games raise IQ | **No.** Gains stay on the trained task (near transfer only). | Simons et al. 2016 (PSPI); Melby-Lervåg & Hulme 2013; Sala & Gobet 2017 |
| Working-memory training (e.g. Cogmed) improves school performance | **No** reliable transfer to reading or math | Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme 2016 |
| Chess or music lessons raise IQ or academic scores | **Effects disappear in well-controlled studies.** Worth doing for their own value. | Sala & Gobet 2016, 2017 |
| "Learning styles" (visual/auditory/kinesthetic matching) | **Not supported.** Use dual coding for everyone instead. | Pashler et al. 2008 (PSPI) |
| "Left-brain / right-brain" learners, "10% of the brain" | **Myths** | Howard-Jones 2014 (Nat Rev Neurosci) |
| Education raises IQ | **Yes**, about 1–5 points per year | Ritchie & Tucker-Drob 2018 (Psych Sci) |
| Spatial skills are trainable with durable effects that transfer | **Yes**, mean g ≈ 0.47 | Uttal et al. 2013 (Psych Bull) |
| Systematic phonics beats whole-language or "three-cueing" | **Yes**, d ≈ 0.41, strongest in K–1 | National Reading Panel 2000; Ehri et al. 2001; Rose Review 2006 |
| Playing linear number board games improves number sense | **Yes**, in RCTs with preschoolers | Siegler & Ramani 2008, 2009 |
| Retrieval practice and spacing help young children too | **Yes** | Fazio & Marsh 2019; Dunlosky et al. 2013 |
| Pure unguided "discovery" learning | **Worse than guided discovery** | Mayer 2004; Alfieri et al. 2011 |
| Toddlers learn language from screens as well as from people | **No.** Social interaction "gates" early language learning. | Kuhl, Tsao & Liu 2003 (PNAS) |

**Design implication:** do not market or design anything as "IQ training". Build cumulative knowledge, fluent foundational skills and executive function through **real, varied, progressively harder tasks**. Diamond's conclusion is that EF gains come from activities that keep challenging the child, not from repeating one drill.

## 3. How learning works (the learning engine)

Stanislas Dehaene (Collège de France), drawing on cognitive neuroscience, describes **four pillars of learning** (*How We Learn*, 2020):

| Pillar | What the brain needs | How the app provides it |
|---|---|---|
| **Attention** | One focal target, low distraction | One concept per screen, no decorative animation during a task, audio instructions for pre-readers |
| **Active engagement** | Predicting and generating, not watching | The child acts (taps, drags, says, builds) every 5–10 s. Ask her to predict before revealing ("What will happen if…?"). |
| **Error feedback** | Immediate, informative, not punishing | Show *why* an answer is wrong (e.g. hear the sound she chose next to the target sound). No "fail" sounds or lives lost. |
| **Consolidation** | Spacing, sleep, retrieval | Spaced-repetition scheduler. Short daily sessions instead of long ones. Review items from earlier days. |

### 3.1 Learning-science mechanisms, ranked by evidence

Utility ratings are from Dunlosky et al. 2013, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*.

1. **Retrieval practice (testing effect): high utility.** Recalling an answer strengthens memory more than re-studying it (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006). This has been shown in young children too (Fazio & Marsh, 2019). The app should *ask* far more than it *shows*.
2. **Distributed (spaced) practice: high utility.** The best gap grows with how long the memory must last (Cepeda et al., 2008). Use an expanding schedule (e.g. 1 day → 3 → 7 → 16 → 35) adjusted per item by accuracy, similar to the Leitner and SM-2 algorithms.
3. **Interleaving.** Mixing problem types beats blocking for discrimination and long-term retention (Rohrer & Taylor, 2007). Example: once *b* and *d* are both introduced, mix them.
4. **Mastery learning.** Move on only after mastery (~80–90%). One-to-one mastery tutoring produced the famous "2-sigma" effect (Bloom, 1984). Mastery learning meta-analytic effect is about 0.5 SD (Kulik et al., 1990).
5. **Optimal difficulty (zone of proximal development, Vygotsky).** Aim for about **80–85% success**. This matches the computational "85% rule" (Wilson et al., 2019) and, more importantly, the motivation research: success that feels earned.
6. **Dual coding and multimedia principles.** Pair words with pictures. Narration is better than on-screen text for pre-readers (modality principle). Remove decoration that does not teach (coherence principle) (Mayer, *Multimedia Learning*; Paivio).
7. **Worked examples and cognitive-load management.** Show, then fade the support, then let her do it alone (Sweller, 1988; Renkl).
8. **Elaboration and self-explanation.** "Why does that work?" prompts, used from age 6+ (Chi et al., 1994).
9. **Metacognition.** Simple "How sure are you?" and "What helped you?" prompts. The Education Endowment Foundation (EEF, UK) rates metacognition among its highest-impact, lowest-cost approaches.

### 3.2 Curiosity: the science behind "curiosity-based learning"

- **Information-gap theory** (Loewenstein, 1994): curiosity is triggered when a child knows *enough to notice a gap*. Design pattern: show a puzzle or mystery first, then let her discover the rule.
- **Curiosity improves memory**, even for unrelated information encountered while curious. It does this through dopaminergic and hippocampal circuits (Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath, 2014, *Neuron*; Kidd & Hayden, 2015).
- **Children as scientists** (Gopnik; Schulz): preschoolers form and test causal hypotheses and explore *more* when evidence is confounding or surprising (Schulz & Bonawitz, 2007). Design pattern: **Predict → Try → Observe → Explain**.
- **Guidance matters.** *Guided* discovery outperforms both direct instruction alone and unassisted discovery (Alfieri et al., 2011). Curiosity sets the hook and structure makes the learning stick.
- **Hidden knowledge suppresses exploration.** When an adult demonstrates one function of a toy, children explore its other functions less (Bonawitz et al., 2011). Leave some things for her to discover.

### 3.3 Motivation: why rewards must be designed carefully

- **Self-determination theory** (Deci & Ryan): durable motivation needs **autonomy** (choice), **competence** (visible progress) and **relatedness** (sharing with a parent).
- **Overjustification effect:** expected, tangible rewards for an activity the child already enjoys *lower* her later intrinsic interest (Lepper, Greene & Nisbett, 1973; meta-analysis Deci, Koestner & Ryan, 1999).
- **Design rules that follow:**
  - **Informational feedback over controlling rewards.** Say "You read 12 new words!" rather than giving coins.
  - **Unexpected celebrations** instead of pay-per-task.
  - A **growing world or collection that shows mastery**, such as a garden that blooms as letters are mastered.
  - **Praise effort and strategy, not "smart"** (Mueller & Dweck, 1998). Note: whole-school "growth mindset" interventions show only small average effects (Sisk et al., 2018). Treat this as hygiene, not a cure.
- **Avoid:** ads, in-app purchases, loot boxes, "streak lost" guilt, timers that pressure a 4-year-old, and autoplay. Radesky et al. (2022, *JAMA Network Open*) documented these dark patterns as widespread in children's apps.

## 4. Educational apps and screens: what the research says

**Hirsh-Pasek et al. (2015), "Putting Education in Educational Apps"** gives four pillars, and the app should meet all four:

1. **Active:** the child is minds-on, not just tapping.
2. **Engaged:** no distracting features. Takacs, Swart & Bus (2015) found that interactive "hotspots" and games inside e-books *reduce* comprehension. Animations that match the story help.
3. **Meaningful:** connected to her real life (her name, family, home objects, her city).
4. **Socially interactive:** parent co-play prompts built in.

**Screen-time guidance:**
- WHO (2019) for ages 3–4: no more than 1 hour of sedentary screen time a day ("less is better").
- AAP (2016): about 1 hour/day of high-quality content for ages 2–5, co-viewed with a parent. For ages 6+, set consistent limits that protect sleep, physical activity and play.

**Practical budget:**

| Age | App time | Off-screen companion activities |
|---|---|---|
| 4–5 | 10–15 min/session, max ~20–30 min/day | 20+ min |
| 6–7 | 15–20 min/session, ~30 min/day | Yes |
| 8–10 | 20–30 min/day | Yes |

**Handwriting must be physical.** Printing letters by hand activates the reading network in pre-literate children; typing and tracing do not activate it in the same way (James & Engelhardt, 2012). The app should *prompt* paper tasks, not replace them.

**Video deficit and social gating.** Young children learn less from screens than from live interaction (Kuhl et al., 2003). Contingent, responsive interaction such as video chat narrows the gap. That is why the app should talk *with* her (respond to her actions) rather than play passive video, and should hand off to a parent regularly.

## 5. Domain 1: Language and literacy (highest priority, ages 4–8)

### 5.1 The scientific model of reading

- **Simple View of Reading** (Gough & Tunmer, 1986): *Reading Comprehension = Decoding × Language Comprehension*. If either is zero, comprehension is zero, so both strands must be built at once.
- **Scarborough's Reading Rope** (2001):
  - **Word recognition strands** become increasingly automatic: phonological awareness → decoding → sight recognition.
  - **Language comprehension strands** become increasingly strategic: background knowledge, vocabulary, language structures, verbal reasoning, literacy knowledge.
- **Ehri's phases of word reading** (pre-alphabetic → partial → full → consolidated alphabetic). Words become instant "sight words" through **orthographic mapping**: connecting each letter to its sound in that word, not memorising whole-word shapes (Ehri, 2014).
- **Neuroscience** (Dehaene, *Reading in the Brain*, 2009): reading recycles the visual word-form area. Grapheme–phoneme training is what wires it. This is why systematic phonics works.

**Expert consensus:** National Reading Panel (USA, 2000), Rose Review (UK, 2006), Australian National Inquiry (2005) and the EEF all recommend **systematic synthetic phonics** in the early years.

### 5.2 English literacy scope and sequence

**Phonological → phonemic awareness** (oral, can start immediately at 4.2):
- rhyme → syllable clapping → onset–rime → **phoneme isolation** (first sound) → blending → segmenting → manipulation (delete or substitute)
- Phonemic awareness taught *with letters* has the largest effect (Ehri et al., 2001, NRP).

**Synthetic phonics sequence** (following established programmes such as *Letters and Sounds* and the Jolly Phonics order):

| Phase | Typical age | Content |
|---|---|---|
| A | 4.0–4.6 | Phonemic awareness and **s a t p i n**, then m d g o c k ck e u r h b f l. Oral blending: "c-a-t → cat" |
| B | 4.6–5.0 | Read and spell CVC words, a handful of "tricky words" (the, I, to, no, go) taught by mapping, not memorising shape |
| C | 5.0–5.6 | Digraphs: sh ch th ng, ai ee igh oa oo ar or ur ow oi ear air er; CVCC/CCVC words (frog, milk) |
| D | 5.6–6.5 | Alternative spellings (ay/a-e/ai), split digraphs, suffixes -s -ed -ing, two-syllable words |
| E | 6.5–8 | Morphology (prefixes, suffixes, roots), spelling rules, multi-syllable decoding |
| F | 8–10 | Greek/Latin roots, etymology, academic vocabulary, fluent reading of complex text |

**Decodable books:** in phases A–D she reads only texts made of the code already taught. This prevents guessing from pictures. The "three-cueing" approach is discredited.

**Fluency:**
- Repeated reading with modelling (NRP 2000).
- In-app: hear a model sentence, read it aloud, re-read it.
- Speech recognition can give feedback from about age 6, but current child ASR is imperfect, so keep a parent in the loop.

**Vocabulary:**
- Robust, explicit teaching of "Tier 2" words such as *enormous, curious, rescue* (Beck, McKeown & Kucan, *Bringing Words to Life*).
- 6–10 new words per week, each met in multiple contexts with spaced retrieval.

**Comprehension and knowledge:**
- Background knowledge drives comprehension. In Recht & Leslie (1988), weak readers who knew baseball out-comprehended strong readers who did not. So build a **knowledge-rich curriculum** (animals, space, body, India and the world, history stories), not generic "skills" passages.
- Teach comprehension strategies (predict, question, summarise, visualise) from about age 7. They give a fast, modest gain (Willingham & Lovette, 2014).

**Oral language and dialogic reading:**
- Dialogic reading (Whitehurst) is a parent technique known as **PEER** (Prompt, Evaluate, Expand, Repeat). It has strong evidence for expressive vocabulary at 3–5.
- The app should *coach the parent* with 2–3 prompts per storybook.

**Writing:**
- **Handwriting:** letter formation on paper at 4–6 (James & Engelhardt, 2012).
- **Spelling** with phoneme–grapheme mapping.
- **Sentence building**, then short composition, from age 6+.
- Keyboarding from about 8.

### 5.3 Hindi (Devanagari)

This is relevant if the family language is Hindi. The repository's existing app is Hindi/UP-focused, so this is assumed but should be confirmed.

**Devanagari is an *alphasyllabary* (akshara-based), not an alphabet.** Research by Sonali Nag and Margaret Snowling (Oxford) on Indian akshara scripts shows:
- Children need **akshara knowledge plus phonemic awareness of the vowel diacritics (matras)**.
- The symbol set is large (~400+ commonly used aksharas, including conjuncts).
- Mastery therefore takes longer than English letter–sound learning, typically **through about Class 4** for full conjunct mastery (Nag, 2007; Nag & Snowling, 2012).

**Sequence:**

| Step | Content |
|---|---|
| 1 | Oral Hindi vocabulary and rhymes (कविताएँ) |
| 2 | Vowels (स्वर) and high-frequency consonants (व्यंजन) as consonant + inherent /a/ |
| 3 | Matras (barakhadi) taught *systematically*: क का कि की कु कू… |
| 4 | Two- and three-akshara words without matras (कमल, घर) |
| 5 | Words with matras |
| 6 | Half-letters and conjuncts (क्ष, त्र, ज्ञ, र् forms) |
| 7 | Fluency and comprehension |

**Policy context (India):**
- **NEP 2020** recommends mother tongue or home language as the medium of instruction to at least Grade 5 where possible.
- **NCF for the Foundational Stage (2022)** and **NIPUN Bharat (2021)** define foundational literacy and numeracy targets through Grade 3.
- Aligning the app with NIPUN's grade-wise learning outcomes keeps it in step with her school.

### 5.4 Second and third languages

- Young children acquire **phonology** best through early exposure (Kuhl). Grammar and vocabulary learning remain strong at later ages too, so there is no "too late" at 10.
- **Bilingualism does not confuse children.** The claimed "bilingual executive-function advantage" is contested and probably small (Paap & Greenberg, 2013; Lehtonen et al., 2018), so value the language itself, not a cognitive side-effect.
- **Social gating** (Kuhl et al., 2003): screen exposure alone is weak for a *new* language at young ages. Pair the app with a human speaker (songs, video calls, a tutor).
- **Recommended order:**
  1. Home language (Hindi) plus English to literacy first.
  2. A third language (e.g. Sanskrit, a regional language, or a foreign language) orally from 5–6, with reading from 7–8.

## 6. Domain 2: Mathematics (high priority; strongest predictor)

**Why early:**
- **Kindergarten-entry math skills predict later achievement more strongly than early reading or attention** (Duncan et al., 2007, a six-dataset analysis).
- **Fraction and division knowledge at age 10 predicts algebra and overall math at 16**, controlling for IQ and family background (Siegler et al., 2012).

**The brain's number system:**
- Humans have an innate **approximate number system** and a "number sense" in the intraparietal sulcus (Dehaene, *The Number Sense*).
- The **triple-code model** links quantities, number words and Arabic digits. Early math is about **binding these three codes** and mapping them onto a **mental number line**.

**Best-evidence practices:**
- **Learning trajectories** (Clements & Sarama, *Building Blocks*). A cluster-RCT showed large effects for preschoolers (Clements & Sarama, 2008). Use their developmental sequences as the backbone of the math curriculum.
- **Linear number board games.** Playing a simple 1–10 linear race game for about 1 hour total (four 15–20 min sessions) improved preschoolers' number-line estimation, magnitude comparison and counting, and the gains held 9 weeks later (Siegler & Ramani, 2008, 2009). A *circular* board did not work. **This is cheap, fun and proven, and should be in the app from day 1.**
- **Concrete → Pictorial → Abstract (CPA).** Bruner's progression, central to Singapore math. Use virtual manipulatives plus *real* objects (beads, coins, blocks).
- **Number bonds and part–whole thinking** (Singapore math), then place value with bundling (tens frames, base-ten blocks).
- **Fact fluency through retrieval and spacing** after understanding, not before. Low-stakes, not timed-and-punitive (Boaler raises concerns about timed-test anxiety; retrieval practice itself is beneficial).
- **Number line as the unifying representation** for whole numbers, then fractions and decimals (Siegler's integrated theory of numerical development).
- **Patterning** (repeating → growing patterns) at 4–6 predicts later math (Rittle-Johnson et al.).
- **Word problems** with bar models (Singapore) from about age 7.
- **Math talk:** parents' number talk at home predicts children's number knowledge (Levine et al., 2010). The app can prompt it ("Count the steps to the door").

### 6.1 Math scope and sequence

| Age | Strand |
|---|---|
| 4–5 | Subitizing to 5; counting principles (one-to-one, stable order, cardinality: Gelman & Gallistel); compare more/less; numerals 0–10; shapes; AB/ABB patterns; linear board game 1–10 |
| 5–6 | Counting to 100; number line 0–20; composing/decomposing to 10 (number bonds); add/subtract within 10 with objects; measurement by comparison; positional language |
| 6–7 | Place value (tens/ones); add/subtract within 100; skip counting 2/5/10; time (hour/half); money; halves/quarters; data with pictographs |
| 7–8 | Three-digit place value; multiplication as equal groups or arrays; division as sharing; unit fractions on the number line; measurement with units; bar-model word problems |
| 8–9 | Times tables to 10×10 (fluency via spaced retrieval); fractions (equivalence, comparison on the number line); perimeter/area; angles; multi-step problems |
| 9–10 | Multi-digit multiplication/division; fraction operations; decimals and place value; negative numbers on the number line; early algebraic thinking (unknowns, function machines); coordinates |

## 7. Domain 3: Spatial reasoning and logic

**Spatial reasoning (strong evidence for malleability):**
- Spatial training effects are moderate and durable, and transfer to untrained spatial tasks (Uttal et al., 2013, 217 studies).
- Spatial skill predicts STEM attainment independently of verbal and math ability (Wai, Lubinski & Benbow, 2009).
- Block play and puzzle play at 2–4 predict later spatial skill (Levine et al., 2012; Verdine et al., 2014).

| Age | Activities |
|---|---|
| 4–6 | Shape puzzles, tangrams, block-copying from pictures, mental rotation of simple shapes, "where is it?" spatial language (above, behind, between) |
| 6–8 | Pattern blocks, symmetry, nets of cubes, simple maps and routes, perspective-taking |
| 8–10 | 3D rotation, cross-sections, coordinate grids, map scale, origami (with paper) |

**Logical and abstract reasoning:**
- **Patterns, classification, seriation** (Piagetian tasks, still valid as curriculum).
- **Analogies** (A:B :: C:?). Relational reasoning develops strongly at 4–10 (Gentner; Goswami).
- **Matrix-style puzzles** are useful as varied problem-solving practice. Gains are domain-specific; do not frame them as raising IQ.

**Computational thinking:**
- ScratchJr-style block coding at 5–7, then Scratch at 8+ (Marina Bers, Tufts / MIT Media Lab).
- Teaches sequencing, debugging and decomposition.
- Unplugged activities (giving a robot directions) at 4–5.

## 8. Domain 4: Executive functions and self-regulation

- **Why:** childhood self-control (ages 3–11) predicted adult health, wealth and criminality in a 1,000-child cohort, beyond IQ and social class (Moffitt et al., 2011, *PNAS*).
- **What works** (Diamond & Lee, 2011, *Science*; Diamond & Ling, 2016):
  - EF improves when children *repeatedly practise* EF skills at increasing difficulty.
  - The best results come from activities that **also engage emotionally, socially and physically**: martial arts, mindfulness for kids, structured pretend play (Tools of the Mind), and certain curricula.
  - Computerised training (e.g. Cogmed) improves working memory on trained-type tasks only.
- **Design implication:** include EF *games*, but understand them as practice, not a cure. Place them inside meaningful play.
  - **Inhibition:** "Simon says" / go–no-go with animals (tap the cat, not the dog); Stroop-like "day/night" tasks.
  - **Working memory:** remember-the-shopping-list stories, sequence recall of growing length.
  - **Cognitive flexibility:** Dimensional Change Card Sort (sort by colour, then switch to shape).
  - **Planning:** Tower-of-Hanoi-style puzzles, multi-step quests, "make a plan before you start" (Tools of the Mind's play-planning).
- **Off-screen companions** (stronger evidence): freeze dance, pretend play with a plan, cooking together (sequencing and measuring), music with rhythm, and sports.

## 9. Domains 5–8: Science, world knowledge, social-emotional skills, arts and movement

### Science and world knowledge (curiosity engine)
- Use **inquiry with guidance**: question → predict → test → observe → explain (Alfieri et al., 2011).
- Build on **core knowledge domains** children reason about early (Spelke; Carey): physics (objects, forces), biology (living vs. non-living, growth), psychology (others' minds), number, space.
- **Knowledge-rich units** (each about 2–4 weeks, revisited spirally):
  - My body, animals and habitats, plants, weather and seasons
  - Light and shadow, floating and sinking, magnets
  - Earth and space, simple machines
  - India and the world map, festivals and cultures, history as stories
- **Bonus:** these units feed reading comprehension directly (Section 5.2).
- **"Why?" bank:** let her ask questions, answered with short narrated visuals, then a follow-up "What do you think would happen if…?"

### Social-emotional learning (SEL)
- School SEL programmes produce gains in social skills and an **11-percentile-point gain in academic achievement** (Durlak et al., 2011, meta-analysis of 213 programs).
- **In-app:** emotion naming (an emotion vocabulary per RULER, Brackett, Yale), perspective-taking stories, calm-down breathing (short, playful).

### Music and arts
- **Include for their intrinsic value.** Rhythm also links to phonological awareness.
- **Do not claim IQ gains:** far-transfer evidence is null in controlled studies (Sala & Gobet, 2017).

### Movement
- Physical activity supports EF and attention. WHO (2019) recommends **at least 180 min/day of varied physical activity for ages 3–4, including 60 min moderate-to-vigorous**.
- The app should send her *out* of the app: movement breaks, nature scavenger hunts.

### Sleep
- Ages 3–5 need 10–13 h; ages 6–12 need 9–12 h (AASM consensus, Paruthi et al., 2016).
- Memory consolidation depends on sleep. **No app use within 1 hour of bedtime.**

## 10. From research to app design

### 10.1 Pedagogical architecture

```
                 ┌─────────────────────────────────────┐
                 │  Knowledge map (skills graph)        │  ← learning trajectories
                 │  e.g. "s-a-t phonemes" → "CVC blend"  │    (Clements & Sarama; phonics phases)
                 └───────────────┬─────────────────────┘
                                 │ prerequisites
┌──────────────┐   ┌─────────────▼─────────────┐   ┌───────────────────────┐
│ Learner model │◄──│  Adaptive engine           │──►│ Activity generator     │
│ per-skill      │   │  - mastery threshold 80-90%│   │ (game templates ×     │
│ mastery prob.  │   │  - target ~80-85% success  │   │  content items)        │
│ (BKT / Elo)    │   │  - interleave due reviews  │   └───────────────────────┘
└──────────────┘   │  - spaced scheduler (SM-2) │
                    └─────────────┬─────────────┘
                                  │
                ┌─────────────────▼──────────────────┐
                │ Session (10-20 min):                 │
                │ warm-up review → new learning →       │
                │ mixed practice → curiosity mystery →  │
                │ off-screen mission card for parent    │
                └──────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Learner model:**
- **Bayesian Knowledge Tracing** (Corbett & Anderson, 1995) or an Elo-style rating per skill.
- Both are standard and well-validated in intelligent tutoring systems.
- Both are simple enough to run fully offline on the phone.

### 10.2 Non-negotiable design rules, derived from the evidence

1. **Audio-first for ages 4–6.** Every instruction is spoken, with no reading required. Use a **clear human voice** recorded for phonemes: pure sounds, "sss" not "suh".
2. **One learning goal per activity.** No decorative animation during a response (coherence principle).
3. **Respond within 1 second, with informative feedback** that shows the correct mapping.
4. **No penalties, lives, timers or ads** for ages 4–7. Gentle optional timing for fact fluency from 8+.
5. **Spaced review is automatic.** About 30–50% of each session is review of due items.
6. **Mastery gates with choice.** She chooses *which* unlocked quest to play (autonomy), but cannot skip prerequisites.
7. **Curiosity hooks.** Each session opens with a mystery ("Why did the shadow get longer?") resolved through the activity.
8. **Parent loop.**
   - Daily **"Play together" card** (2–3 dialogic-reading prompts, a math-talk prompt, a movement or EF game).
   - Weekly 1-page progress view in plain language.
   - Clear guidance on *what to do off-screen*.
9. **Physical-world tasks.** Letter formation on paper, real blocks/tangrams, counting real objects, nature walks. The app asks; the parent confirms.
10. **Session limits built in.** A friendly "Time to play outside!" stop; a daily cap set by the parent; a bedtime lockout.
11. **Personalisation for meaning.** Her name, family members and familiar places in stories and word problems.
12. **Privacy.**
    - Offline-first; no third-party trackers; no ads.
    - India's **Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023** requires verifiable parental consent and prohibits tracking or targeted advertising directed at children. The same principles apply under COPPA in the US.
    - Data stays on the device unless the parent exports it.

### 10.3 Game templates (reusable mechanics × content)

| Template | Used for | Evidence anchor |
|---|---|---|
| **Sound Safari:** hear a sound, pick the picture/letter | phonemic awareness, grapheme–phoneme | NRP phonemic awareness + letters |
| **Blend Train:** sound carriages join into a word | blending/decoding | synthetic phonics |
| **Word Builder:** drag letters to spell a dictated word | segmenting/spelling, orthographic mapping | Ehri |
| **Story Cave:** decodable book with read-along, then 2 questions | fluency, comprehension | repeated reading; Takacs et al. (no distracting hotspots) |
| **Number Race:** linear 1–10 → 1–100 board game | number line, magnitude | Siegler & Ramani |
| **Bond Builder:** split and join quantities with ten-frames | part–whole, addition | Singapore CPA |
| **Line Leap:** place numbers or fractions on a number line | magnitude estimation | Siegler |
| **Shape Shifter:** tangrams, rotation, block copy | spatial | Uttal et al. |
| **Robot Path:** sequence arrows/commands | computational thinking, planning | Bers |
| **Animal Rules:** go–no-go, rule-switching sort | EF (inhibition, flexibility) | Diamond; DCCS |
| **Mystery Lab:** predict → test → explain | science inquiry, curiosity | Alfieri; Schulz |
| **Feelings Friends:** name the emotion, choose a strategy | SEL | Durlak; RULER |

### 10.4 Existing products: what to learn from them

These are useful references for UX, but **none has strong independent RCT evidence across the whole 4–10 span**. Treat them as design inspiration, not proof.

- **Teach Your Monster to Read** (Usborne Foundation): phonics-sequenced games.
- **Khan Academy Kids:** broad, free, no ads.
- **Duolingo ABC:** short phonics lessons.
- **DragonBox:** hides algebra in a game.
- **ScratchJr:** coding.
- **GraphoGame:** grapheme–phoneme drills. The EEF-funded UK trial of GraphoGame Rime found **no significant impact on reading** compared with business-as-usual. A drill-only app is not enough without teaching and reading of real text.
